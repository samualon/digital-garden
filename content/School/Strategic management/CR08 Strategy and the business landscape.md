# Introduction
There exist substantial differences in firm performance between industries, but within industries even more susbtantial differences in firm performance are observed.

To understand these within-industry differences, a closer look as to be taken at the competitive landscape that these firms are in.

The reading will first review historical methods of analyzing competitive position, then it will touch upon the process of analyzing competitive position.
# Development of analyzing competitive position
Competitors within an industry can be grouped based upon their performance, strategy characteristics and interactions. Three methods of analyzing these firms are laid out:
1. [[Cost analysis]]
2. [[Differentiation analysis]]
3. [[Added value analysis]]
## Cost vs differentiation analysis
The two strategies above are named as **generic strategies**. To maintain consistency inside of the firm, they might opt to choose only one of these strategies and focus solely on these principles. However, dual competitive strategies are possible but rare.

Since some customers might not want an oversimplified or -complex product, there might be a need for an intermediate product. Thus a positioning between cost and differentiation might be needed for certain firms.

Ultimately, the most important thing is the wedge between cost and differentiation. Firms should choose a point in the spectrum where costs and the premia resulting from differentiation are maximized, not either cost or differentiation.
# Activity analysis
This part takes a look at how managers can increase [[Willingness to pay]] more than the costs.

This is done by firstly decomposing the firm into specific activities. Differences in activities can result in more added value compared to other firms by either increasing [[Willingness to pay|WTP]] or reducing costs.

Three steps follow after the distinction of these activities:
1. Use activities to analyze relative costs
2. Use activities to analyze relative [[Willingness to pay|WTP]]
3. Define strategic options and make choices
## Use activities to analyze relative costs
As managers have laid out all activities of the company, they then have to define all the [[Cost driver]]s of these activities.

Managers aren't able to see competitors' costs, but they can observe their cost drivers. This allows comparing with their firms cost drivers and evaluate the competitive position.
### Notes
- A cost driver should be modeled only if it is likely to vary across the competitors or in terms of the strategic options that will be considered.
- As these steps rely on assumptions, a [[Sensitivity analysis]] is necessary.
- Difference in available resources can be a cost driver.
## Use activities to analyze relative WTP
Activities not only impact costs, but also the [[Willingness to pay|WTP]] of customers and thus the [[Economic value added]] and profitability.

[[Willingness to pay|WTP]] can be impacted through all activities, but activities relating to the characteristics of the product have the most impact.

To *calculate* [[Willingness to pay|WTP]], a method of four steps can be used:
1. Define *real* buyers of the product/service
2. Pinpoint what these buyers want
3. Find how successful the firm is at meeting these customer expectations
4. Relate the successfulness of step 3. to the relevant activities
# Define strategic options and make choices
Defining options is a creative task, but there are some guidelines:
- Consider customers' reactions to the strategic changes.
- Evaluate the possible impact of all activities, not only those related to the product/service's characteristics.
- In rapidly developing markets, [[Early adopters]] can be a proxy of what the broader public wants.
- Changing the scope of targeted customers can be the most impactful strategic decision.
